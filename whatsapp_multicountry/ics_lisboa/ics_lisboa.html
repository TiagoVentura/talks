Evidence from a Multi-Country WhatsApp Experiment

Motivation

After early years of optimism, recently the public and academic debate about social media have increasingly focused on its negative consequences on society. Social Media is said to:

  • Facilitates the spread of misinformation (Guess, Nyhan, and Reifler 2018; Flaxman, Goel, and Rao 2016)

  • Facilitates users’ self-selection on online echo chambers (Guess et al. 2023; Sunstein 2018)

  • Increase exposure to uncivil/low-quality content (Bor and Petersen 2022)

  • … therefore polarizing voters around the world (Banks et al. 2021; Tokita, Guess, and Tarnita 2021; Bail et al. 2018; Settle 2018)

  • … and harming mental health (Tromholt 2016; Vanman, Baker, and Tobin 2018; Hanley, Watt, and Coventry 2019)

Challenge 1: Overemphasis on feed-based platforms

  • Information consumption and content propagation varies greatly across different social media platforms (Feed-Based vs Social Messaging Apps vs Non-Social Graph Apps) (Garimella et al., 2019, Resende et al., 2019, Ventura et al., 2024)

Challenge 2: Research is concentrated in a handful of high-income countries:

  • Effects of social media usage are likely to vary across contexts (Budak et al., 2024). For example, between low vs high-income countries:
    • Less content moderation from platforms, and
    • Weaker independent media infra-structure
    • Social Media more heavily used for news consumption.

Challenge 3: Evidence is mostly correlational

Research Agenda

Understand information consumption via WhatsApp in the Global South, and identify causal effects of WhatsApp Usage on political and non-political attitudes.

This is a hard question!

  • Problem I: In a context of widely adoption of certain social media applications, RCTs “treating” users with social media have several theoretical constraints.

    • Solution: Instead of “activating”, researchers have used deactivation designs to measure the short term causal effects of social media usage (Asimovic et al., 2022, Allcott et al., 2020, Arceneaux and Ladd 2023)

  • Problem II: Fully deactivating WhatsApp users further ethical and empirical challenges:

    • Solution: Partial Deactivation ~ induce friction to usage or channels to which users receive “problematic” content on WhatsApp (Ventura et. al., 2025)

Multi-Country WhatsApp Usage Reduction Experiment

  • Design: Usage Reduction -> Friction + Incentive to reduce usage:

    • Time Treatment: Add time limit for WhatsApp usage per day + Limit Usage to 5min per day;
    • Media Treatment: Disable their automatic download of media on WhatsApp, and do not purposefully click and watch any media on WhatsApp;

  • Duration:

    • Treatment: Change settings and behavior for 4 weeks (one month) leading up to election in three countries
    • Control: Change ONLY behavior for 3 days.

  • Multicountry Design: selected some of the largest Global South countries having democratic elections in 2024.

    • India: General Election Spring 2024
    • South Africa: Presidential Election Spring 2024
    • Brazil: Local Election Fall 2024

Theory

On WhatsApp, what you see depends on which chats you’re in

Weak-tie groups

Large, strangers, joined by invite link

Partisan coordination, viral multimedia, quasi-anonymous content

(Gursky et al. 2022; Chauchard & Garimella 2022)

Strong-tie groups

Family, friends, neighbors

Homogeneous, conflict-avoidant: misinformation goes uncorrected

(Zhu et al. 2024; Chadwick et al. 2024)

One-on-one chats

Where most messages go

Least toxic, least political

(Rossini et al. 2025)

Usage reduction affects volume and composition

  • Volume: less time on the app means less content, mechanically

  • Selectivity: a binding constraint makes users pick

    • Which conversations to enter and keep
    • Which content to open inside them

  • Prediction: users hold on to one-on-one and family chats and let go of large weak-tie groups, where toxic content and misinformation circulate

Volume
Intensive margin, lowering consumption		 Selectivity
Reallocating scarce attention
Time
Conversation level		 Limit how often and how long participants use WhatsApp, mechanically reducing all content encountered. Participants decide which conversations merit their limited time, and thus they prioritize one-on-one exchanges.
Multimedia
Content level		 Blurs all photos and videos received, reducing the volume of multimedia participants are exposed to. Participants decide whether to manually download each piece of blurred multimedia, and they are more likely to do so for content from close contacts in one-on-one chats.

Hypotheses

Information consumption

  • Less exposure to low-quality and uncivil political discussion (H1–H2)
  • Less exposure to misinformation (H3a) and true news (H3b)

Belief accuracy

  • Better at spotting false news (H4a), worse at spotting true news (H4b)

Political attitudes

  • Less partisan polarization (H5)
  • Less identity-based prejudice (H6)
  • Less issue polarization (H7)
  • Less extreme candidate favorability (H8)

Non-political (exploratory)

  • Substitution and well-being

Experimental Design

  • Participants enrolled: 2426 (678 in India, 820 in South Africa, and 928 in Brazil)

  • Final Sample: 2220 (653 in India, 742 in South Africa, and 825 in Brazil)

Two ways to add friction without cutting people off

Multimedia

Turn off automatic downloads. Don’t open images, video, or audio.

Time

Set a native app timer. Use WhatsApp 10 minutes a day at most.

The control group took the treatment for three days

  • Controls got the same instructions, for 3 days instead of 4 weeks, with no settings change

  • Why:

    • Screens out never-takers from both groups
    • Makes it harder for controls to know they’re controls
    • Keeps attrition balanced

  • Standard in deactivation studies (Allcott et al. 2020, 2024; Arceneaux et al. 2023; Ventura et al. 2025)

  • Treatment assignment is revealed only after the baseline screenshot

We checked compliance with weekly screenshots

Multimedia arm: bytes of media received

Time arm: daily WhatsApp screen time

Baseline plus four weekly screenshots, from treatment and control alike

Treated users cut usage in every country and in both arms

  • Time: 47% of treated stayed under 10 min/day for all four weeks, vs. 16% of controls
  • Multimedia: 77% of treated consumed less media than the average control
  • Multimedia was easier to comply with. Even a 10-minute cap is hard for most people.

Results

Measuring exposure to news and misinformation

  • Misinformation: 4 false stories that went viral during each campaign, picked from national fact-checkers

  • True news: 4 real headlines from major outlets during the same weeks, plus 2 placebo fakes with a key fact reversed

  • For each headline we ask:

    • Have you seen this in the past 30 days? → Recall
    • Is it true? → Accuracy

  • Plus indices of online incivility and low-quality political discussion (Allcott et al. 2020; Rossini 2022)

Less WhatsApp meant less incivility, less misinformation, and less news

  • The trade-off: people lost true news too

Seeing less didn’t change what people believed

Consistent with prior deactivation studies (Allcott et al. 2020, 2024; Ventura et al. 2025)

Measuring political attitudes

  • Partisan polarization: feelings, willingness to interact, and traits for voters of the two main coalitions

    • BJP vs. Congress · ANC vs. DA · PL vs. PT

  • Identity-based prejudice: the same items for Hindus/Muslims (India) and Black/White South Africans

  • Issue polarization: distance from the control-group mean on six campaign issues

  • Candidate favorability: in-party vs. out-party leaders (Modi, Gandhi, Ramaphosa, Zuma, Lula, Bolsonaro, …)

Did less exposure to toxic content and misinformation reduce polarization?

Not quite.

Political attitudes didn’t move

One exception: Time arm in India reduced anti-Muslim prejudice (d = −0.13, p = 0.067)

These nulls match the rest of the literature

Studies that shifted consumption but not attitudes:

  • WhatsApp multimedia deactivation, Brazil 2022 (Ventura et al. 2025)
  • Facebook/Instagram deactivation, US 2020 (Allcott et al. 2024)
  • Chronological instead of algorithmic Facebook feeds (Guess et al. 2023a)
  • Removing reshares from Facebook feeds (Guess et al. 2023b)
  • Reducing like-minded sources on Facebook (Nyhan et al. 2023)

People felt better, and spent the time on hobbies and TV

Well-being gains were largest in Brazil, where people went offline

  • Subjective well-being: +0.14 SD pooled
  • Hobbies +0.22 SD, TV +0.09 SD, other social media −0.10 SD
  • Brazil: biggest well-being gain and biggest shift to offline activities, including time with friends
  • Less screen time alone may not be enough. The benefit seems to come from what replaces it.

Implications

Key Takeaways

  • WhatsApp is an important channel through which voters receive misinformation and news in major Global South democracies

  • Reduced exposure to such information does not mechanically affect accuracy perceptions

  • In the context of elections, WhatsApp also increases exposure to toxic content and uncivil political discussions

    • …none of this change meaningfully political attitudes (at least not within a short electoral period)

  • Yet limiting WhatsApp usage also improves overall subjective well-being, and these effects are associated with higher time spent on offline activities.

Participants found it hard at first, but saw benefits

“It was hard at first and felt like I was missing out on the rest of the day’s interactions, but I got used to it and started feeling like I had so much time on my hands and could do a lot more things.”

– South African participant

“It was a big challenge, the first few days were the hardest, but I kept myself busy with other things. My boyfriend liked that I spoke to him more in person instead of on WhatsApp.”

– Brazilian participant

60% of treated participants described positive experiences, 47% negative ones. Many said both.

Friction by default is a policy that works and can scale

  • Platforms won’t add friction on their own: it cuts engagement

  • Our Multimedia arm is a settings change:

    • WhatsApp could make media downloads opt-in by default
    • Regulators could require screen-time limits in a default digital health toolkit

  • Cheaper and less intrusive than bans, and it preserves access to essential communication

  • Participants said the settings changes themselves helped them cut back

Caveats

  • Sample: online recruits who agreed to cut back, younger and heavier users than the eligible pool
  • Self-reports: exposure and well-being are survey measures
  • Timing: four weeks, at the height of a campaign. Effects may differ at other points in the electoral cycle.
  • Mechanism: we can’t cleanly separate selectivity from volume

Small barriers to WhatsApp use cut exposure to toxic content and misinformation, cost people some news, left their politics unchanged, and made them feel better.
